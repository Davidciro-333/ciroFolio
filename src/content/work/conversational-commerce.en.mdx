> The products and the brand are not named here. This is a pilot: small paid campaigns,
> real conversations, and no sales volume worth quoting yet. Figures as of November 2, 2026.

## The brief

Sell low-priced digital products over WhatsApp to people who arrive from an ad, with nobody on
shift. The model is unusual: the product is delivered first, and the buyer is then invited to
pay what they think it is worth by bank transfer and send the receipt. No checkout, no card
form, no account.

That model only works if three things hold. The conversation has to feel like a person, not a
menu. Delivery has to happen exactly once. And a receipt has to be checked properly, because
a fake one costs nothing to send.

It runs on the self-hosted n8n I operate on AWS, with Redis for memory and locks, a Google
Sheet as the customer state the sales team can read, and alerts to Telegram.

## Decision one: one door to the channel

Everything that talks to WhatsApp lives in a single sub-workflow with a contract that knows
nothing about the provider: type, recipient, text, file, caption, delay. Nothing else in the
system knows which channel it is on.

That looked like over-engineering on day one. It paid for itself three times. The engine has
run on an unofficial WhatsApp bridge, on Telegram for testing, and on an official BSP. Each
move touched about 11 of roughly 84 nodes. The agent, the delivery, the receipts, the sheet
and the follow-ups were not touched once.

## Decision two: the prompt is not a safeguard

A prompt can be argued with. Code cannot. So every rule that would cost money or trust if
broken lives in code:

- **The model never writes payment details.** It emits a tag; the delivery workflow sends
  the account numbers from its own configuration. A model cannot invent an account it never
  sees.
- **The receipt validator gets its facts injected.** A language model has no clock and does
  not know whose account is the right one. Without the date and the account in the prompt, it
  rejected valid receipts and would have accepted transfers to someone else.
- **It never approves on its own when in doubt.** A model error means "I am checking it" to
  the customer and an alert to a human. Two rejections escalate to manual review.
- **Follow-ups are filtered in code:** opted out, already paid, two messages sent, or outside
  the 24-hour window, and the message does not go, whatever the model thinks.

## What production taught me

Most of what is in this engine was found by running it, not by planning it.

**A deploy silently broke every accent.** A configuration change pushed through an old shell
re-encoded every non-ASCII character as a question mark, in a product written in Spanish. It
was caught before any customer saw it, restored character by character from a backup, and
since then every deploy goes through a script that uploads UTF-8 from a file and verifies
the live version is identical to the one that was generated.

**WhatsApp truncated the offer at the worst word.** Long replies get cut with "Read more"
around 700 characters, and the cut landed exactly on the explanation of the pay-after model,
the argument the whole sale rests on. Replies now go out one paragraph per bubble.

**Real customers broke assumptions.** People with usernames instead of phone numbers, messages
the platform reports as unavailable, a repeated "yes" that would have delivered twice. Each one
got a fix and a test conversation before the campaign kept running.

## Swapping the product

In September the first product was retired and a second one took its place. The engine was
updated in place, keeping every workflow id, after a full backup that can be restored with
one command. It was tested live from three numbers the same day.

The honest part: today the product still lives in several workflows: prompts, file ids,
payment accounts, sheet tabs. The next step is already designed: a catalog, so that a new
product is one row and not a careful edit across seven workflows. It is not built, because it
should be shaped by the second brand, not guessed before it exists.

## Where it stands

Running since July 2026, now on its second product with a small paid test campaign. Since
September 30 it has handled 115 real conversations on its own: at ten minutes each, about 19
hours of attention nobody had to give, nights and weekends included. The rule
for reading results is about fifty conversations per ad angle before drawing any conclusion,
and it is not there yet. What it proves today is not revenue. It is that an unattended sales
conversation can run for months without delivering twice, approving a fake receipt, or
messaging someone who said stop.
